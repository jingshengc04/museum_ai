## Age and height
The tree was 1,300 years old and 101 metres tall when it was felled in California.
Source: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/giant-sequoia-slice.html

## Rings
Tree rings form under the bark as a tree grows taller and thicker, and counting them estimates its age.
Conservation work revealed the slice's many rings, which show how long the tree lived.
Source: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/giant-sequoia-slice.html

## At the museum
The slice has been at the museum since 1893 and was on public display for more than a century as part of the
botany collections. It now stands on the upper balcony of Hintze Hall, in the eaves.
Source: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/giant-sequoia-slice.html

## Conservation
Before treatment the top was under a layer of dust about an inch thick, over cloudy old varnish and wax.
It took three conservators 12 weeks to clean and conserve the slice. Once it was clean, they applied a new
conservation-grade resin that is stable and reversible, so it can be removed later if needed.
Source: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/giant-sequoia-slice.html

## Forests then and now
When the sequoia was felled, nearly 70% of the world's land area was covered in trees and forest. After 100
years this had dropped to below 40%, with an estimated 15 billion trees being cut down every year.
Source: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/giant-sequoia-slice.html

## Appearance (for visuals, not narration)
A very large, round cross-section of trunk stood on edge on the top balcony of Hintze Hall, under the hall's
terracotta arches and painted ceiling panels. The cut face is warm reddish-brown wood with hundreds of fine
concentric rings, newly cleaned and glossy; the edge is a thick, fibrous, cinnamon-red bark. A timeline of
history runs beside it. As a living tree: a colossal straight red-barked trunk rising out of a Californian
forest.
